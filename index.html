Overview

Players need to identify when they're in a voice party, know who's speaking, and easily access controls and settings to configure their voice preferences.

Based on competitive research, four product needs shaped every decision: active speaker indication, individual mute and volume control, mute status reassurance (because nobody wants the moment of realising they've been talking on a live mic), and clear interaction signals throughout.

Result: five core components, 14 documented states across 3 platform contexts, adopted by all three game teams and presented to 300+ Rioters at the company-wide capstone.

Visual System

↑ Example of muting someone in your party

Process
01
Console-first component architecture
Five components built for controller input focus states, bumper button navigation, and context menus instead of hover or right-click flows. Party Tab + Legend, Voice Settings, Voice Settings States, Micro Social Panel, Mini Party Panel. Anatomy, states, and guidelines defined for every platform context.
02
Design Team Jungling (Meeting) & engineering feedback loops
First voice settings iteration used a blurred background overlay. Team Jungling and engineer sessions revealed it wasn't working too many competing elements, volume affordance unclear. Rebuilt as a dedicated settings page with a defined access flow: focus player → player card → voice settings. That's the version that shipped.
03
Constraint-aware design
Mute animation required per-frame state sync too costly for console. Went back to the brief: the need was reassurance, not motion. Proposed icon swap and opacity fade. Shipped the icon swap; documented the animation as a future enhancement when performance budgets allowed.
04
Company-wide showcase
Presented the full system to 300+ Rioters at the company-wide capstone brief, component decisions, and every tradeoff distilled into one clear narrative.
Design Iteration — Blurred Overlay → Settings Page

The first iteration placed voice controls directly over the game UI as a blurred background overlay. In design and engineering sessions it became clear this wasn't working — too many competing elements, the volume affordance was unreadable, and the hierarchy broke down on console where there's no hover to guide attention.

The solution was a dedicated settings page reached through a deliberate console flow: party tab → player card → voice settings. Cleaner hierarchy, zero competing elements, built for controller navigation.

↑ Iterations 1 & 2: blurred overlay across two game contexts — competing elements, unclear affordance. Iteration 3: dedicated Voice Settings page. That's the version that shipped.

Component States

↑ Micro Social Panel → Voice Chat States: In Voice, Speaking, Muted. A blue voice ring indicator activates when speaking. A persistent muted icon gives reassurance players are never unsure if their mic is live.

In-Game Party Voice Activity

↑ Activity hierarchy: when a player speaks, their avatar and Riot ID highlight. When they stop, it fades. At a glance, you know who just spoke and who's speaking now no label needed.

In-Game Party Voice Activity–Other States

↑ These states apply when the player wishes to deafened themselves or restrict their microphone activity.

Representation in Social Panel, No Access to Social Panel

↑ When the user is actively in game and does not have access to the full social panel, the Mini Party Panel offers a way for the user to make changes on the go.

Settings Muting

↑ Voice Settings States: slider focused → active, icon appears on adjustment. Slider unfocused → resting. Mute microphone selected → slider disabled. Four states, one component, each with a deliberate trigger. Deeper settings like push-to-talk and mic selection live in the game team's existing settings this page handles in-the-moment controls only.

Reflection

Console was completely new to me I'd never designed for controller input before. That was the real learning curve: no hover states, no right-click, everything navigated through focus and button sequences. The constraint became the most interesting design problem of the internship.

✓ What worked

The design gem and engineer feedback loops were invaluable the blurred background iteration proves it. Designing within real constraints is where the interesting problems live, and having a team willing to push back early saved the direction. Weekly stakeholder meetings forced messy in-progress work into clear, defensible decisions.

→ Next time

I'd document scope boundary decisions earlier specifically why certain controls were kept as in-the-moment settings vs. handed off to the game team's existing settings. Those calls shaped the whole component architecture, and capturing the reasoning at the time would have made the handoff cleaner.

Overview

A full rebrand and design system for Neuron Digital not just a visual refresh, but a scalable brand architecture built to grow with the company across products, platforms, and contexts.

The goal was coherence without rigidity: a system flexible enough to expand into new surfaces while maintaining a clear, ownable visual identity.

Motion & Animation
Motion Design

Process

02
Visual language development
Built the visual system from scratch typography, colour, spacing, iconography, and motion principles that give Neuron Digital a consistent, ownable identity.
03
Component system & documentation
Translated the brand into a scalable UI component library usage guidelines, naming conventions, and documented patterns so any team member could extend the system without breaking coherence.

Overview

Casablanca Records defined 1970s excess home to KISS, Donna Summer, Parliament, and the Village People. A label built on spectacle and fearless self-expression.

The brief: give this brand a bright, electrifying personality that captures disco as a cultural force, not just a sound.

Texture & Atmosphere
Electrifying · Visceral · Alive
Applications
Concept
01
Disco as Futurism
In the 70s, disco was modernity a vision of the future through sequins and strobes. The visual language needed that same charge: electric, luminous, unapologetically bold.
02
The Roster as a Compass
Every decision filtered through the acts: KISS, Donna Summer, Village People, Cher a lineage stretching to Tiësto, SG Lewis, and KUNGS. Spectacle and soul in equal measure.
03
Neon as Identity
Warm, glowing, slightly imperfect the kind of sign burning at 2am that pulls you in from across the street. Neon became the core visual metaphor for everything the label stood for.
Reflection

Working on a real cultural artifact forced me to think about what a brand feels like, not just what it looks like. The 70s constraint pushed me toward a bolder, more atmospheric visual language than I'd normally reach for.

✓ What worked

Leading with emotion and era rather than trying to modernise the brand. The neon treatment gave the identity an immediacy that felt true to the label's personality.

→ Next time

I'd push further into the full system how does this identity live on merchandise, packaging, and digital? The sign is a strong anchor; I'd want to build more surfaces around it.

Reflection

My professor challenged me to design a brand that held real political weight without feeling heavy-handed. The visual language had to feel energised and accessible something that says "this is serious, and it's going to be fun."

✓ What worked

Anchoring the identity in the five PLAY-in values gave every design decision a clear filter. The neon-green accent and bold type system made the brand instantly readable at poster scale and thumbnail scale alike.

→ Next time

I'd develop the motion system earlier the speaker slides and logo animation shaped a lot of the static work, and building those first would have tightened the overall coherence.
